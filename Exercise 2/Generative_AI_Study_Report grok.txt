================================================================================
1. ABSTRACT
================================================================================

Generative AI can speed up learning in core computing modules if it is used as
a tutor, critic, and drill generator — not as a substitute for thinking. This
report explains how a university student can use large language models to
understand concepts, practise problems, find mistakes, plan study, and prepare
for examinations in Probability and Statistics, Database Management Systems
(DBMS), and Software Engineering. It also covers advantages, limitations,
risks of over-reliance, and a weekly method that keeps the student in charge.

The governing rule is simple: the student must remain the reasoner. AI helps
when it forces explanation, comparison, and correction. It harms learning when
finished answers are copied, trusted without checking, or used to skip the work
in which understanding is actually formed.


================================================================================
2. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

These three subjects sit at the centre of most computer science and information
technology degrees. They suit generative AI because they mix dense ideas
(independence, normalisation, cohesion), formal work (proofs, SQL, UML, design
patterns), and a large amount of practice. They are also dangerous. The same
tool that explains Bayes’ theorem can invent a wrong formula, write SQL that
runs but answers the wrong question, or produce a design that looks professional
and cannot be defended in a viva or closed-book exam.

This report assumes the reader is taking these courses, has access to a capable
chatbot, and must follow an academic integrity policy. AI should not replace
lectures, textbooks, or tutors. Used with discipline, it can make those
resources more effective.


================================================================================
3. A RESPONSIBLE WAY TO USE AI
================================================================================

3.1 Four steps that keep you in charge

Use this loop for every topic:

1. Attempt the work first, on paper or in your own editor.
2. Ask the model to explain, quiz, or critique — not to replace the attempt.
3. Check the output against lecture notes, the textbook, or a small example
   you compute yourself.
4. Re-solve or re-explain with the chat closed.

If you skip the first step, you are only consuming text. If you skip checking,
errors spread. If you skip re-solving, you will not remember it. A useful test:
could I teach this to a classmate tomorrow without opening the chat? If not,
the tool was a crutch, not a tutor.

3.2 Academic integrity

Most universities allow AI for explanation, extra practice, and critique of
your own draft, if the module says so. They forbid submitting AI answers, code,
or reports as your own; using AI in a banned exam; and pasting assessed work
into a model and handing in the output. Rules differ by module. Permission in
a software-engineering project is not permission in a statistics test. Read the
handbook. When unsure, ask the lecturer in writing. Keep a short log: date,
tool, what you asked, what you kept.

3.3 How to prompt

Weak prompt: “Explain normalisation.”

Stronger prompt: “I understand 1NF and 2NF. Using the relation
Marks(student_id, course_id, student_name, course_name, grade, lecturer), show
why it is not in 3NF, decompose it, then quiz me. Do not give answers until I
reply.”

The second version gives your level, a concrete example, a task, and a check.
That is how you get teaching instead of a generic paragraph.

4. PROBABILITY AND STATISTICS
================================================================================

AI is good at new explanations and unlimited practice questions. It is
unreliable as a silent algebra engine and as a source of p-values unless you
also compute them yourself.

4.1 Understanding concepts

Ask for several views of the same idea: the course definition, a small
numerical example, a common mistake, and a counter-example. After a lecture,
paste your own notes and ask where you are vague. For Bayes’ theorem, use real
counts (1,000 people, 1% prevalence, 90% sensitivity, 5% false-positive rate),
compute the posterior yourself, then compare. For the Central Limit Theorem,
ask why “n = 30” is a slogan, not a theorem, and when the approximation is slow.

Sample prompt: Explain a 95% confidence interval at first-course level. Quiz me
until I stop saying it means “the probability that the true mean lies in the
interval”. Then give two unseen numerical problems, no answers.

4.2 Practising problems and finding mistakes

Ask for problems only. Solve on paper. Then request a mark scheme. Useful
drills: the same P(A|B) from a table, a tree, and Bayes; deciding whether a
setup is binomial, Poisson, or neither; reading a hypothesis-test printout and
stating H0, H1, the unchecked assumption, and whether the conclusion is causal.
Always re-do one textbook question whose answer you already trust. If the model
disagrees with the book, the book wins until you know why.

Paste your full working, including the wrong answer. Ask for the first
incorrect line, the name of the misconception, a one-line fix, and a similar
problem. Typical student errors: using P(A and B) = P(A)P(B) without
independence; mixing P(data | H0) with P(H0 | data); using a z-interval when
σ is unknown and n is small; treating a non-significant result as “the null is
true”. Typical AI errors: invented formulae, a dropped Jacobian, a p-value of 0.

Sample prompt: Generate five unseen problems on tests for a mean, mixed z and
t. Include one where I should refuse the test because an assumption fails. No
answers. Mark my work as a strict examiner.

4.3 Study plans, exams, and example

Give the model your weeks left, hours per week, weak topics, and whether a
formula sheet is allowed. Demand concept days, timed practice, mixed retrieval,
and one past-paper question a week with the chat closed. Cut the plan until it
is followable. Near the exam, ask for a paper of the same shape as yours. Do
not ask for a whole-module summary the night before.

Example. Disease prevalence 0.01, sensitivity 0.9, false-positive rate 0.05.
You compute P(disease | +). The model flags that you used 0.05 as
P(+ | disease). You repair: P(+) = 0.9×0.01 + 0.05×0.99 = 0.0585, so
P(D | +) = 0.009 / 0.0585 ≈ 0.154. You then solve a spam-filter version timed,
and write the one-sentence interpretation a marker wants. Attempt, find the
error, repair, transfer, phrase: that is the method.

5. DATABASE MANAGEMENT SYSTEMS
================================================================================

DBMS mixes theory (relational algebra, functional dependencies, normalisation,
transactions) with craft (SQL, indexing, schema design). AI is a good partner
for both, and a weak source of truth for vendor-specific SQL and for “this ER
diagram is complete”.

5.1 Understanding concepts

Keep the layers separate: conceptual (entities), logical (relations, keys, what
3NF forbids), physical (indexes), language (algebra to SQL), and runtime (what
repeatable read still allows). Give a relation and a set of FDs, ask for
candidate keys, then recompute closures yourself. Do not ask “what is 3NF?”
Ask whether this relation is in 2NF, 3NF, or BCNF, and whether a decomposition
can be both lossless and dependency-preserving. Translate π, σ, ⋈, ÷ to SQL and
back. Draw a precedence graph yourself.

Sample prompt: Using FDs {A→B, B→C, DE→A}, find all candidate keys of
R(A,B,C,D,E). Show closures. Is R in BCNF? Then stop. I will try the
decomposition before you show it.

5.2 Practising problems and finding mistakes

Reuse one small schema (Student, Course, Enrolment) for weeks. Ask for queries
of rising difficulty, for English that you must turn into SQL, and for
ambiguous English so you notice the ambiguity. Draft an ER model from a short
brief, then ask the model to attack missing keys and wrong cardinalities. Run
every SQL answer on a real engine. Execution is the check AI cannot replace.

Paste your SQL or decomposition. Ask whether it answers the specification or a
nearby different question, how it treats NULLs and empty tables, and what test
data would make it fail. Common student errors: INNER JOIN when LEFT JOIN is
required; a non-grouped column in SELECT; WHERE instead of HAVING; confusing
2NF with 3NF; lossy decomposition. Common AI errors: MySQL syntax in a
PostgreSQL course; claiming 3NF while a hidden FD remains; invented query plans.

Sample prompt: List each course and the number of students, including courses
with zero students. My SQL: SELECT c.name, COUNT(*) FROM Course c JOIN
Enrolment e ON c.id = e.course_id GROUP BY c.name; Do not rewrite it. What
happens for an empty course, and which two changes fix it?

5.3 Study plans, exams, and example

Interleave theory and SQL. A five-week block can run: relational model; daily
query drills; FDs and normalisation; transactions and indexing; mixed papers.
Practise 2-mark definitions (superkey versus candidate key; lossless-join
versus dependency preservation). If SQL is on paper, practise mental execution
on four-row tables.

Example. Students and courses are many-to-many; each enrolment has a grade;
each course has exactly one lecturer. You draft entities. You ask AI only to
interrogate, not to fix. It asks whether a student can enrol twice and whether
a course can exist without a lecturer. You add keys, Course.lecturer_id NOT
NULL, and unique (student_id, course_id). You write CREATE TABLE statements,
then run INSERTs that should fail. The learning is in the decisions, not in
generated DDL.

6. SOFTWARE ENGINEERING
================================================================================

Software engineering is about framing the problem, quality under constraints,
and talking to humans. AI is already used in industry, which makes it both a
fair study tool and a serious integrity risk in coursework.

6.1 Understanding concepts

Do not use AI as a glossary. Use it to force trade-offs into the open. Ask
which process model is least inappropriate for a mobile app, a medical device,
and a research prototype — and what fails if you pick the fashionable one.
Paste a vague brief and let the model interview you; then you extract
functional versus non-functional requirements. Demand that cohesion, coupling,
and SOLID be explained on a short code example, not as slogans. For each design
pattern in the syllabus, state the problem, one good fit, and one misuse, then
add a second misuse yourself.

Sample prompt: Take a module that talks to the database and also formats HTML
email. Show a refactor that improves cohesion, and what pain remains. Ask me to
name the principle before you name it.

6.2 Practising problems and finding mistakes

Turn a one-page brief into actors, use cases, a class diagram, and three
non-functional tests. Write a small feature yourself, then ask for a review of
naming, error handling, and testability — not cleverness. Design equivalence
partitions before code exists. Paste your failing test and stack trace; you
investigate the causes. Review your artefacts, not the model’s. Ask which tests
would still pass if the code always returned constants.

Student mistakes: requirements that describe screens instead of capability;
class diagrams that are only a database schema; tests that copy the code;
microservices for a small CRUD project. AI mistakes: unjustified patterns,
imaginary APIs, extra layers you cannot explain in a viva, generic security
advice that misses the actual bug. If a coding assistant is allowed, you must
still explain every submitted line.

Sample prompt: Story: import a CSV of modules. Write nothing. Interview me
until acceptance criteria are testable. I will write them; you will mark
missing failure cases (duplicates, encoding, partial failure).

6.3 Study plans, exams, and example

Plan by assessed outcomes, not chapter titles. Practise one diagram or one
requirements page twice a week. For scenario exam questions use a skeleton:
stakeholders, conflicts, options, trade-off against stated quality attributes,
recommendation plus risk. Compute cyclomatic complexity by hand. For a viva,
have the model play a hostile examiner: timeouts, invariants, what you
personally implemented. In group work, use AI to critique your minutes, not to
invent a contribution you did not make.

Example. Clinic appointments. You write a one-page problem statement without
AI. An interview shows you had not defined overbooking, cancellation windows,
or who may see another patient’s notes. You write use cases and NFRs, choose a
simple layered design, implement a thin slice, test double-booking first, and
accept only review comments you can defend. The AI never builds the system. It
shortens the loop between your decisions and a critique of them.


================================================================================
7. ADVANTAGES, LIMITATIONS, AND RISKS
================================================================================

7.1 Advantages

- Patient re-explanation after a lecture that did not land.
- Unlimited extra questions, including near-transfer items that exams reward.
- Diagnosis from your wrong solution, which a printed mark scheme cannot do.
- Help turning a module outline into a calendar you then cut down.
- Practice at the phrasing markers want, and at directing tools you will meet
  at work.

7.2 Limitations

The writing is confident when the BCNF test or the design pattern is wrong. The
model does not know your lecturer’s conventions. Long derivations often hide a
silent error in the middle. It cannot see your query planner or your tests
unless you paste evidence. Long chats forget earlier constraints. It prefers
fashionable software stacks that are poor coursework answers. It cannot sit the
exam. When the model and the prescribed textbook disagree, follow the textbook
and the lecturer.

7.3 Risks of over-reliance

Reading a perfect explanation feels like knowing it; closed-book retrieval is
the honest test. If the model always chooses the statistical test, the join, or
the architecture, you never practise the choice that papers assess. Skipping
the struggle of a lossless decomposition skips the lesson. Unacknowledged AI
text or code can be treated as plagiarism. A viva that asks you to walk through
a proof, a query, or a function will expose copy-paste more harshly than a thin
but honest attempt. Pasting unpublished exam questions, identifiable project
data, or internship code into a public model can breach exam rules,
data-protection law, or contract.

Table 1. What over-reliance damages first

Subject                      | What atrophies first                          | How it shows up
-----------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------
Probability & Statistics     | Modelling judgement and careful conditioning | Can run a t-test, cannot say if data are paired
DBMS                         | Mental execution of queries and FD reasoning  | SQL looks right but returns duplicates
Software Engineering         | Design justification and debugging            | Pattern soup; cannot explain a commit in a viva

8. WEEKLY METHOD AND READY-TO-USE PROMPTS
================================================================================

Four sessions of 50–90 minutes beat one panicked night.

(A) Same day as the lecture: rewrite it from memory, then ask what questions
    would expose that you only copied slide titles.
(B) Attempt set exercises unaided; use AI only after a serious try, and only
    to find the first error or to request a similar item.
(C) Sit a short mixed quiz with no answers, timed; log errors by type
    (independence, LEFT JOIN, untestable requirement), not by chapter.
(D) Teach the week’s idea in five minutes, or write “when this tool applies”.

Weekly cap: one past-paper question, exam conditions, chat closed.

Prompts that transfer across all three subjects:

- “Here is my solution. Find the first incorrect step. Stop there.”
- “Problems only. Wait for my answers before marking.”
- “Mark this as a strict unseen paper. Do not be kind.”
- “Same idea, new story, numbers changed, one extra twist.”
- “Ask me questions. If I am vague, do not correct me; ask a sharper question.”
- “Use only PostgreSQL / our lecture’s definition of 3NF. If unsure, say so.”
- “Give a small counter-example showing that statement S is false.”

Starter prompts by subject:

Probability:
“I have just studied [topic]. I will write 8 lines. Ask five questions, waiting
after each. Then two unseen numerical problems, no answers. Flag any step that
needs independence.”

DBMS:
“Here is a schema and FDs. I will find keys and the highest normal form. Check
me from the first error. Then four SQL tasks, including one outer join and one
‘for all’. No answers yet.”

Software engineering:
“Here is a 150-word brief. Interview me until requirements are testable. I will
produce use cases and a three-layer design; review missing failure cases. Then
viva me as the examiner.”


================================================================================
9. INTEGRITY CHECKLIST AND CONCLUSION
================================================================================

Before any assessed work, answer these in writing:

- Does this module’s policy allow this use? If unclear, have I asked?
- Did I attempt the substance before prompting?
- Can I reproduce the result with the tool closed?
- Have I checked formulae, SQL, or citations against a trusted source?
- Have I removed personal or unpublished data from prompts?
- If I used AI, have I acknowledged it in the form the school requires?
- Could I walk an examiner through every paragraph and every line of code?

If a deadline is driving you to paste the whole coursework into a model, that
is a planning failure, not a tooling opportunity. Ask for a 24-hour breakdown,
then do the work. Sleep still outperforms another hour of generated text.

Conclusion. Generative AI is a strong companion when used to explain, drill,
critique, and plan, and a liability when used to produce answers you will
submit. In probability and statistics, multiply examples and catch conditioning
mistakes; do not outsource derivations or p-values. In DBMS, interrogate
schemas and drill queries; do not trust unverified SQL or normal-form claims.
In software engineering, use it for interviews, review, and viva practice; do
not submit a system you cannot defend.

Students who gain from these tools still work problems on paper, still run SQL,
still write tests, and still sit past papers with the chat window closed. The
AI only makes those hours more targeted. That is the standard this report
recommends: not because the tools are weak, but because the subjects are real,
and the degree certifies that you can do the work.
