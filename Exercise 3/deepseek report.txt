Using Generative AI Effectively and Responsibly in Probability and Statistics, Database Management Systems, and Software Engineering

Introduction

Generative AI tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and similar large language models are becoming widely available to university students. When used thoughtfully, they can act as on-demand tutors, practice partners, and study planners. When used carelessly, however, they can produce incorrect information, encourage shallow learning, and create academic integrity problems.

This report explains how a university student can use generative AI effectively and responsibly in three subjects: Probability and Statistics, Database Management Systems, and Software Engineering. For each subject, the report covers five areas: understanding concepts, practising problems, identifying mistakes, creating study plans, and preparing for examinations. It also discusses advantages, limitations, risks of over-reliance, and practical examples.

A central principle applies throughout: generative AI should be used as a learning aid, not as a replacement for your own reasoning, textbooks, lectures, or practical experience.

1. Probability and Statistics

Probability and Statistics requires both conceptual understanding and computational skill. Students often struggle with abstract ideas such as randomness, distributions, hypothesis testing, and Bayesian reasoning. Generative AI can help make these ideas more concrete.

1.1 Understanding Concepts

Generative AI can explain statistical concepts in multiple ways, using analogies, simple examples, and step-by-step derivations.

For example, if you are confused about the Central Limit Theorem, you can ask:

"Explain the Central Limit Theorem intuitively, then give a formal statement, then show a simple simulation example in Python."

The AI might explain that the CLT says the distribution of sample means becomes approximately normal as sample size increases, even if the original population is not normal. It can then generate Python code using numpy and matplotlib to simulate rolling dice or drawing from an exponential distribution.

Other useful concept-related prompts include:

- "Explain the difference between frequentist and Bayesian probability with an example."
- "What is a p-value, and what are common misunderstandings about it?"
- "Why do we divide by n-1 in sample variance?"
- "Explain conditional probability using a medical testing example."

A strong approach is to ask the AI to explain a concept at different levels: beginner, intermediate, and advanced. This helps you identify gaps in your understanding.

1.2 Practising Problems

Generative AI can generate unlimited practice problems with solutions. You can request questions on a specific topic and at a specific difficulty level.

Example prompt:

"Generate five practice problems on the normal distribution. Include one on finding probabilities, one on inverse probabilities, one on the sampling distribution of the mean, and two word problems. Provide detailed solutions."

You can also ask the AI to create problems that mirror your course style. For example:

"My course uses notation like X ~ N(mu, sigma squared). Create three exam-style questions on confidence intervals for a population mean."

For probability, you can ask the AI to write short simulation code to check your answers. For instance:

"Write R code to simulate 10,000 rolls of two dice and estimate the probability that the sum is 7. Compare this with the theoretical probability."

This connects theoretical work with computational intuition.

1.3 Identifying Mistakes

One of the most valuable uses of generative AI is error checking. If you have attempted a problem and are unsure about your solution, you can paste your reasoning into the AI and ask it to identify mistakes.

Example prompt:

"Here is my solution to a hypothesis test. I am testing H0: mu = 100 against H1: mu > 100. My sample mean is 103, sample standard deviation is 10, and n = 25. I got a p-value of 0.038 and rejected H0 at alpha = 0.01. Is this correct? If not, where did I go wrong?"

The AI can point out that a p-value of 0.038 is larger than 0.01, so you should not reject the null hypothesis at that significance level. It may also check your test statistic and degrees of freedom.

You can also ask the AI to list common mistakes on a topic. For example:

"What are the most common errors students make when interpreting confidence intervals?"

This helps you avoid typical pitfalls.

1.4 Creating Study Plans

Generative AI can help you organise your study time. Provide the AI with your syllabus, exam date, available study hours, and current confidence level. It can then produce a weekly plan.

Example prompt:

"I have a Probability and Statistics midterm in four weeks. Topics include descriptive statistics, probability rules, discrete and continuous distributions, sampling distributions, confidence intervals, and hypothesis testing. I can study six hours per week. Create a four-week study plan with specific topics, practice tasks, and review sessions."

The AI can produce a plan that allocates time proportionally to each topic and includes spaced repetition. You should still adjust the plan based on your own progress.

1.5 Preparing for Examinations

Generative AI can create mock exams, generate formula sheets, and quiz you using flashcards.

Example prompt:

"Create a 60-minute mock exam for an introductory statistics course. Include five multiple-choice questions, three short-answer questions, and two longer problems on confidence intervals and hypothesis testing. Provide a marking scheme."

You can also ask:

"Quiz me on statistical definitions. Ask me one question at a time and wait for my answer."

This interactive mode is useful for active recall.

1.6 Advantages, Limitations, and Risks

Advantages

- Available 24/7 and can provide immediate explanations.
- Can generate an unlimited number of practice questions.
- Can adapt explanations to your level.
- Can help connect theory with code through simulations.
- Can identify arithmetic or conceptual mistakes in your work.

Limitations

- May perform incorrect calculations, especially for complex probability problems.
- May misuse statistical language or give misleading interpretations.
- May generate unrealistic datasets or oversimplified examples.
- Cannot replace a human tutor's judgement or a lecturer's specific emphasis.

Risks of over-reliance

- You may accept incorrect answers without checking them.
- You may lose the ability to solve problems independently.
- You may use AI to complete graded assignments, which is usually academic misconduct.
- You may develop false confidence because AI explanations feel clear even when they are wrong.

1.7 Practical Example Prompts for Probability and Statistics

- "Explain Bayes' theorem using a spam email example."
- "Generate a dataset of 100 observations and ask me to compute the mean, median, and standard deviation. Then check my answers."
- "I don't understand why the expected value of a geometric distribution is 1/p. Show me a derivation and an intuitive explanation."
- "Create five multiple-choice questions on sampling distributions."
- "Here is my regression output. Help me interpret the R-squared, p-value, and slope coefficient."
- "What are the assumptions of a two-sample t-test, and how do I check them?"

2. Database Management Systems

Database Management Systems combines theory with practical skills such as SQL, database design, normalisation, transactions, and indexing. Generative AI can be especially helpful for practising SQL and understanding abstract design principles.

2.1 Understanding Concepts

DBMS concepts can be abstract. Generative AI can explain topics such as normalisation, ACID properties, indexing, and concurrency control using simple analogies.

Example prompt:

"Explain ACID properties in databases using a bank transfer example."

The AI might explain atomicity by saying that either both the debit and credit happen or neither does; consistency means the total money remains correct; isolation means another transaction cannot see a half-finished transfer; durability means the transfer survives a system crash.

Other useful prompts:

- "What is the difference between 3NF and BCNF? Give an example where a relation is in 3NF but not BCNF."
- "Explain how a B+ tree index works and why it is useful for range queries."
- "What is the difference between a clustered and non-clustered index?"
- "Explain the difference between logical and physical data independence."

You can also ask the AI to convert an ER diagram description into relational schemas, or vice versa.

2.2 Practising Problems

Generative AI can generate SQL practice questions based on a schema you provide.

Example prompt:

"Here is a schema: customers(customer_id, name, city), orders(order_id, customer_id, order_date), order_items(order_id, product_id, quantity), products(product_id, name, price). Generate ten SQL query questions, ranging from basic SELECT to joins and aggregation. Provide answers."

The AI can produce questions such as:

- Find all customers from London.
- Find the total amount spent by each customer.
- Find the top three products by quantity sold.

You can also practise normalisation. For example:

"Here is a relation: student_courses(student_id, student_name, course_id, course_name, instructor_name, instructor_office). Normalise it to 3NF and explain each step."

For transaction management, you can ask:

"Generate three transaction schedules and ask me whether they are conflict serialisable. Then provide the precedence graph and answer."

2.3 Identifying Mistakes

If your SQL query returns an error or incorrect results, you can paste it into the AI and ask for help.

Example prompt:

"My SQL query is supposed to find customers who have never placed an order, but it returns an empty result. Here is the query: SELECT c.name FROM customers c JOIN orders o ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id WHERE o.order_id IS NULL; What is wrong?"

The AI can explain that using a JOIN removes customers without orders, and that you should use LEFT JOIN or NOT EXISTS.

You can also ask the AI to review your ER diagram or relational schema for design problems.

Example prompt:

"Here is my relational schema for a library database. Identify any design problems, such as redundancy, update anomalies, or missing foreign keys."

2.4 Creating Study Plans

DBMS courses usually cover relational algebra, SQL, ER modelling, normalisation, transaction management, and indexing. You can ask the AI to create a study plan based on your syllabus.

Example prompt:

"I have a DBMS final exam in five weeks. Topics: ER modelling, relational model, SQL, normalisation up to BCNF, transactions and concurrency control, and indexing. I can study eight hours per week. Create a study plan with weekly goals, practice tasks, and review sessions."

The AI can suggest combining theory reading with hands-on SQL practice, since DBMS is a practical subject.

2.5 Preparing for Examinations

Generative AI can create mock exams with a mix of theory and SQL questions.

Example prompt:

"Create a DBMS mock exam with five SQL questions, two normalisation questions, two short theory questions on transactions, and one ER diagram design question. Provide a marking scheme and sample answers."

You can also ask for summaries of key definitions:

"Create a one-page revision sheet covering SQL joins, normal forms, ACID properties, and indexing."

For active recall, ask:

"Quiz me on DBMS terminology. Ask one question at a time."

2.6 Advantages, Limitations, and Risks

Advantages

- Excellent for practising SQL because it can generate many query variations.
- Can explain database design principles using concrete examples.
- Can review your SQL queries and suggest corrections.
- Can generate ER diagram descriptions and relational schemas.
- Can summarise large amounts of theory quickly.

Limitations

- SQL syntax may vary between database systems such as MySQL, PostgreSQL, Oracle, and SQL Server. AI-generated SQL may not work on your specific system.
- AI may hallucinate functions or features that do not exist.
- Complex transaction schedules and concurrency problems may be analysed incorrectly.
- Text descriptions of ER diagrams may be ambiguous or incomplete.
- It cannot run SQL queries itself unless connected to a tool, so it cannot always verify actual output.

Risks of over-reliance

- If you always ask the AI to write SQL, you will not develop query-writing skill.
- You may not learn to debug database errors independently.
- Using AI to complete graded SQL assignments is likely to violate academic integrity policies.
- You may accept an AI-generated normalisation that is not actually correct.

2.7 Practical Example Prompts for DBMS

- "Explain the difference between WHERE and HAVING in SQL with examples."
- "Write a SQL query to find the second highest salary from an employee table. Explain how it works."
- "What is a deadlock? Give an example involving two transactions."
- "Normalise this relation to 3NF: employee_projects(emp_id, emp_name, dept, project_id, project_name, hours)."
- "What is a composite index, and when should I use one?"
- "Here is my SQL query. It is slow. Suggest possible indexing or rewriting improvements."
- "Explain the difference between COMMIT and ROLLBACK."

3. Software Engineering

Software Engineering covers process models, requirements engineering, software design, UML, design patterns, testing, project management, and architecture. Generative AI can help explain these concepts, generate examples, and review your design or code.

3.1 Understanding Concepts

Software engineering concepts can be broad and overlapping. Generative AI can clarify differences and give practical examples.

Example prompt:

"Explain the difference between agile and waterfall software development. Include advantages, disadvantages, and an example project suitable for each."

The AI can compare the two approaches and help you understand why agile is preferred for projects with changing requirements, while waterfall may work for well-defined, regulatory projects.

Other useful prompts:

- "Explain the SOLID principles with a simple example for each."
- "What is the difference between white-box and black-box testing?"
- "Explain the MVC architectural pattern using a simple web application example."
- "What is the difference between verification and validation?"
- "Explain the difference between a use case and a user story."

You can also ask the AI to generate UML diagrams using tools such as PlantUML or Mermaid.

Example prompt:

"Create a class diagram in PlantUML for a simple university registration system. Include classes for Student, Course, Registration, and Professor, with appropriate relationships."

This gives you a visual starting point that you can then refine.

3.2 Practising Problems

Generative AI can create software engineering practice tasks, from requirements analysis to design and testing.

Example prompt:

"Given the following requirements for a library management system, write three user stories with acceptance criteria, identify the main functional and non-functional requirements, and propose a high-level architecture."

The AI can generate a structured answer that you can compare with your own.

For design patterns, you can ask:

"Give me a coding scenario where the Observer pattern would be useful. Show a short Java or Python implementation."

For testing, ask:

"Here is a short function. Generate a set of unit test cases using boundary value analysis and equivalence partitioning."

You can also ask the AI to suggest refactoring improvements for a piece of code.

3.3 Identifying Mistakes

Software engineering involves many design decisions. Generative AI can review your work and suggest improvements.

Example prompt:

"Here is a Python class I wrote for a shopping cart. Review it for SOLID violations, code smells, and possible bugs. Suggest refactoring."

The AI may point out that the class has too many responsibilities, that it violates the single responsibility principle, or that some methods are hard to test.

You can also ask for a requirements critique:

"Here are my user stories for an e-commerce app. Are they too vague? Do they follow the INVEST criteria? Suggest improvements."

For UML diagrams, you can describe your diagram and ask:

"Here is a description of my class diagram. Does the relationship between Order and Customer make sense? Should it be composition or aggregation?"

3.4 Creating Study Plans

Software engineering courses often include both theory and project work. You can ask the AI to create a plan that balances reading, design practice, and revision.

Example prompt:

"I have a software engineering final exam in six weeks. Topics include SDLC models, requirements engineering, UML diagrams, design patterns, testing, and project management. I can study seven hours per week. Create a six-week study plan with specific activities and revision."

The AI can suggest drawing UML diagrams by hand, reviewing design patterns with code examples, and practising test case design.

3.5 Preparing for Examinations

Generative AI can create mock exam questions and help you practise answering them.

Example prompt:

"Create a software engineering mock exam with five short-answer questions on agile, UML, testing, and design patterns, plus one long design question. Provide a marking guide."

You can also ask:

"Give me a scenario and ask me to draw a use case diagram and a class diagram. Then check my description against a model answer."

For memorising terminology, ask:

"Quiz me on software engineering definitions, such as cohesion, coupling, refactoring, and technical debt."

3.6 Advantages, Limitations, and Risks

Advantages

- Can generate clear examples of design patterns and architectural styles.
- Can review code and design for common problems.
- Can help you structure requirements and user stories.
- Can produce UML diagrams in text-based formats.
- Can explain trade-offs between different approaches.

Limitations

- AI-generated code may contain bugs, security issues, or poor style.
- It may oversimplify real-world constraints such as performance, team dynamics, and legacy systems.
- UML diagrams generated as text may not fully capture the nuances of a design.
- It may present outdated practices or generic advice.
- It cannot replace hands-on experience with real projects and version control.

Risks of over-reliance

- If you ask the AI to write code or design documents for you, you will not develop your own engineering judgement.
- You may accept an AI-generated architecture that is unsuitable for the actual problem.
- You may not learn to debug or think critically about trade-offs.
- Using AI for graded design or coding assignments without permission may breach academic integrity rules.

3.7 Practical Example Prompts for Software Engineering

- "Explain the difference between composition and inheritance. When should I prefer composition?"
- "What is the difference between unit testing and integration testing? Give examples."
- "Here is a short Python function. Generate five unit tests using pytest."
- "What is technical debt? How can a team manage it?"
- "Create a use case diagram description for an online food ordering system."
- "Review my simple class design for a banking application and suggest how to reduce coupling."
- "Explain the repository pattern and when it is useful."
- "What are the main activities in the software requirements engineering process?"

4. Cross-Cutting Responsible Use Guidelines

Regardless of the subject, the following practices will help you use generative AI responsibly:

1. Attempt the problem first. AI is most useful after you have tried to solve a problem yourself. If you always ask for the answer first, you will not learn.

2. Ask for reasoning, not just answers. Use prompts like "Explain your reasoning step by step" or "Show me how you reached this answer." This helps you learn the process.

3. Verify AI outputs. AI can be confidently wrong. Cross-check facts with textbooks, lecture notes, official documentation, or by running code yourself. For SQL, run the query in a real database. For statistics, verify calculations with a calculator or script.

4. Be aware of academic integrity. Many universities allow AI for brainstorming or study help but prohibit using it to complete graded work unless explicitly permitted. Check your course policy. If you use AI to generate ideas or text, cite or acknowledge it as required.

5. Protect privacy and confidentiality. Do not paste personal data, other students' work, unpublished research, or confidential project material into public AI tools.

6. Use AI to supplement, not replace, primary resources. Your lecture notes, recommended textbooks, and practical labs should remain your main study materials. AI can clarify and provide extra practice, but it may not match your course's specific terminology or emphasis.

7. Develop metacognition. After using AI, ask yourself: "Could I now explain this without AI? Could I solve a similar problem on my own?" If the answer is no, revisit the topic.

8. Keep a prompt log. Saving useful prompts and AI responses can help you revise later. However, do not treat AI responses as authoritative notes without checking them.

5. Conclusion

Generative AI is a powerful tool for university students studying Probability and Statistics, Database Management Systems, and Software Engineering. It can explain difficult concepts, generate unlimited practice problems, identify errors, create personalised study plans, and simulate exam conditions. In Probability and Statistics, it can clarify abstract statistical ideas and generate simulation code. In Database Management Systems, it is particularly useful for practising SQL and normalisation. In Software Engineering, it can help with design patterns, requirements, code review, and UML modelling.

However, generative AI is not infallible. It can hallucinate, produce incorrect calculations, generate invalid SQL, oversimplify software engineering trade-offs, and give misleading statistical interpretations. Over-reliance can weaken your problem-solving skills, create false confidence, and lead to academic misconduct.

The most effective approach is to treat generative AI as a tutor or study partner: use it to ask questions, test your understanding, and receive feedback, while always doing your own thinking and verifying important results. When used responsibly, generative AI can deepen your understanding and make your study time more efficient without replacing the hard work that real learning requires.
